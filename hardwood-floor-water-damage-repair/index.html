What water does to a hardwood floor.

Hardwood is a natural material that swells when it takes on moisture. Boards usually cup first, with the edges higher than the center. If the water is severe or long-lasting, boards can crown, buckle off the subfloor or separate at the seams.

Finish does not protect against standing water. Water gets in through seams, edges and any gap, and it can sit between the boards and the subfloor where you cannot see it.

Sanding, refinishing or replacing.

Minor cupping often settles as the floor dries, and a later sanding and refinish can restore the surface. Severe buckling or boards that have separated usually mean replacement of the affected area. We tell you which case you have after we measure, and we put the repair scope in writing.

Do not sand or refinish a floor that is still wet. Finishing over moisture leads to peeling, staining and trapped dampness.

Common causes we see.

  • Plumbing leaks and appliance failures. See appliance leak damage.
  • Slab and subfloor moisture. See slab leak water damage.
  • Storm and roof leaks that run down walls to the floor.
  • Flooding from outside. See flood damage restoration.

Why hardwood needs its own drying plan.

Wood dries from the surface inward, and drying it too fast can make cupping worse. Too slow and the underside stays wet long enough to grow mold. Floor drying equipment lets us pull moisture at a controlled rate, often by sealing the floor under a mat and drawing air through the boards.

Installation matters as much as the water. A nailed floor over a plywood subfloor behaves differently from a glued floor over concrete, and a floating engineered floor can often be lifted and dried in sections. We look at how your floor was installed before we choose a method.

We also check the baseboards, the wall bottoms and the cabinet bases around the room. Water that wicked into those areas can keep feeding the floor edges long after the boards look dry.
