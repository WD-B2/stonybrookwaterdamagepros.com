Water damage help for Strathmore homes.

Most water damage in a single-family house starts inside the walls or under a fixture. A supply line splits, a water heater tank gives way, a dishwasher hose lets go or a roof leak soaks the ceiling below. When that happens in Strathmore, our dispatch line is the place to start.

Call, tell us where the water is and whether it is still running, and we plan the first steps with you. Then the crew works through extraction, drying and a written scope you can show your insurer.

Where Strathmore is and how it began.

In April 1963 Levitt and Sons announced Strathmore at Stony Brook, planned as 1,300 single-family homes on 650 rolling wooded acres about a mile southeast of the historic village. The Three Village Historical Society notes that the new SUNY Stony Brook campus had opened in September 1962, and Levitt saw the two as helping each other.

Sales opened on the weekend of November 16, 1963, with six model homes west of Nicolls Road off Route 347, which that article spells Nichols. A Union College collection dates the Strathmore houses to April 1963 through 1966. That puts most of the neighborhood at roughly sixty years old.

What the public record does not tell us.

We found no public source that says how many Strathmore homes have basements, which ones sit on slabs, or which are on sewers instead of septic systems. We also have not verified FEMA flood-zone status for the neighborhood. We say so plainly, because guessing would send you the wrong advice.

Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status is not verified for this page. To check one address, search it on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center, and ask the Town of Brookhaven Division of Building about local requirements.

The first hour in a single-family house.

These steps buy time until a crew arrives. Take only the ones that are safe for you.

Stop the source.

Close the main water valve or the supply to the appliance if you can reach it without standing in water.

Keep power and water apart.

Do not use a breaker panel or outlets near standing water. If you are unsure, stay out and call a licensed electrician or the utility.

Photograph before you move things.

Take wide and close photos of the wet areas, then lift valuables and papers out of the water.

Call the line.

Give the dispatcher your street, where the water is and whether it looks clean, dirty or like sewage.

Know your drain before a backup happens.

Whether a house sends wastewater to a sewer or to a cesspool or septic tank changes the cleanup. Suffolk County states in its Septic Improvement Program FAQ that 74% of county residents rely on antiquated cesspools and septic systems. If you do not know which you have, find out now rather than during a backup.

A sewage backup is contaminated water. Keep children and pets out, do not run fans in the room and do not try to vacuum it. Our sewage backup cleanup page explains what protected removal looks like.

Why drying matters more than cleanup speed alone.

Wiping a floor does not finish the job. Water moves into drywall, insulation, subfloors and the bottom of cabinets, where it stays. Air movers and dehumidifiers pull that moisture out while meters track the readings until the materials reach their drying goals.

That record, with photos and moisture readings, is also what makes a claim easier to explain. See our structural drying page for how the process works.

Insurance records after a leak.

The New York State Department of Financial Services advises policyholders to file claims promptly, photograph damage before cleanup, make only temporary repairs until the insurer inspects and keep an inventory and a log. Coverage depends on the cause of the loss and your policy.

We keep photos, moisture readings and drying logs so the record is clear. You remain in charge of how you work with your insurer, and we do not decide coverage.
