How to Detect Mold in Your Home After Water Damage.

Where mold hides after a leak, what signs to look for and when to call a licensed assessor.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Mold shows itself through sight, smell and history. Look for dark, green, white or fuzzy patches on walls, ceilings, baseboards and around windows. Notice a persistent musty odor. Think about any past leak, flood or damp carpet that was not dried quickly. If you suspect mold behind a wall or in a large area, a licensed mold assessor can evaluate it, and in New York the assessor and remediator must be separate for jobs over 10 square feet.

This guide explains where mold hides after water damage and how to decide on next steps. It does not make health claims and it does not replace a licensed assessment.

Start with the water history.

Mold needs moisture. The most useful question is not whether you can see mold but whether something in the building got wet and stayed wet. A pipe leak that was dried in a day is a very different story from a basement carpet that stayed damp for a week.

Write down what you know: when the water event happened, what was wet, how long it took to dry and whether the source was fixed. A restoration crew can add moisture readings, which show whether the building is dry now.

Visual clues.

Spots and patches.

Black, green, gray or white growth that may look fuzzy or powdery on walls, ceilings and trim.

Stains and discoloration.

Yellow or brown marks, often in rings, where water has traveled through drywall.

Peeling or bubbling paint.

Moisture behind the surface lifts the finish.

Warped or soft materials.

Baseboards, drywall and subfloor that swell or crumble.

Smell clues.

A persistent musty smell with no obvious source often points to hidden dampness. It can come from insulation, a wall cavity, a crawl space or under carpet. The odor may be stronger when the heat or air conditioning runs or when humidity is high.

Tools that actually help.

Moisture meters show whether materials are wet, and thermal cameras show temperature differences that can point to moisture. Neither tool identifies mold. They help find the damp conditions mold needs. A licensed assessor decides what testing is appropriate.

Home test kits are sold in stores, but they cannot tell you how much mold is present or whether it is a concern. For a decision that affects remediation, rely on a licensed assessor.

What New York requires.

The New York State Department of Labor runs the mold program. For mold areas over 10 square feet, licensed professionals are required, and the assessor and the remediator must be separate. A written remediation plan comes first, and a post-remediation clearance check follows the work. The Department offers a lookup tool for licensed contractors.

What to do if you suspect mold.

  • Find and stop the moisture source..
  • Dry wet materials as soon as possible..
  • Do not disturb suspect areas more than needed..
  • Photograph what you see and note the dates..
  • If the area is large or hidden, contact a licensed assessor..

Mold and health questions.

We do not make health claims about mold. If someone in your home has health concerns, talk to a doctor. A physician can advise you on symptoms and what to do.

For the drying and repair side, see our page on mold remediation and structural drying.

How long to wait before worrying.

Mold can begin on damp materials within a couple of days, but a single day of dampness that was dried fully is a very different situation from a long-term leak. The point is not to panic. It is to check, measure and act on what you find.

A room-by-room walk-through.

Start in the basement or lowest level, where moisture collects. Look at the base of walls, behind stored boxes and around the water heater and laundry area. Move to bathrooms and check around tubs, showers, windows and under sinks. In kitchens, look beneath the sink, behind the dishwasher and around the refrigerator.

Then go upstairs and check ceilings under bathrooms and roof lines, plus window sills and exterior walls. In the attic, look at the underside of the roof deck, around vents and chimneys and at the insulation for dark or damp patches. Take photos and notes so you can compare later.

Humidity and condensation checks.

High indoor humidity supports mold even without a leak. Windows that fog or drip, a musty bathroom that stays damp after showers and cold walls with beaded water are all signs. A simple hygrometer shows indoor humidity. Ventilation, exhaust fans and a dehumidifier help in humid spaces.

If humidity is high and you also have a past leak, the two problems feed each other. Fixing both is the way to stop mold from returning.

A short list of what to write down.

  • The room, the wall or ceiling and the approximate size of the affected area..
  • The date you first noticed it and any water event before that..
  • Whether the smell is stronger at certain times of day or in certain weather..
  • Photos with a ruler or coin for scale..

This record helps a licensed assessor, a plumber and your insurer understand the situation without having to guess.

Sources and standards.

This guide draws on the references below. Check them directly for the current rules that apply to you.

  • New York State Department of Labor mold program: licensing, written remediation plans and clearance checks.
  • ANSI/IICRC S520, the published standard for professional mold remediation, cited as an industry reference.
