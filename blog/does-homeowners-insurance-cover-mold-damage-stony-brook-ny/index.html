Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Mold Damage?

Why the cause of mold decides coverage, what limits policies set and how New York mold rules fit in.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Mold coverage depends on the cause and on your policy. Many homeowners policies cover mold only when it results from a covered event, such as a sudden pipe burst, and many cap the amount they will pay. Mold from long-term leaks, humidity or neglect is commonly excluded. Read the mold language in your policy or ask your agent for a clear answer.

This guide explains how insurers generally look at mold claims, what documentation helps and how New York mold rules fit in. It is general information, not insurance or legal advice.

Why the cause matters.

Insurers tend to ask what caused the moisture that led to mold. If a covered sudden water loss caused it, the mold that follows may be covered, often with a limit. If the mold came from a slow leak, ongoing condensation or a flood that your policy excludes, coverage is less likely.

That is why drying a loss quickly is important for both the building and the claim. Fast mitigation can reduce the chance that mold develops at all. See our guide on water extraction and structural drying for how that works.

Common policy features to look for.

Mold-related policy terms.
Term.What it often means.What to ask.
Mold exclusion.No coverage for mold, or only if caused by a covered peril.What perils count?
Sublimit.A cap on mold payments.What is the dollar cap?
Ensuing loss.Mold that follows a covered loss.Is there a time limit?
Maintenance exclusion.Excludes neglect or long-term seepage.How is long-term defined?
Flood exclusion.Standard policies exclude flood.Do I have separate flood coverage?

Flood, storm and sewer backup.

The New York State Department of Financial Services notes that flood damage is generally covered only by flood insurance. Mold that results from a flood may fall under the flood policy, whose terms are different from a homeowners policy. Sewer or drain backup is often an optional endorsement.

What helps a mold claim.

  • Documentation of the original water event, including date and cause..
  • Photographs of the damage and the mold before cleanup..
  • Records showing how quickly the area was dried..
  • Reports from licensed mold professionals where required..
  • A written log of your communications with the insurer..

How New York mold rules interact with a claim.

The New York State Department of Labor requires licensed professionals for mold areas over 10 square feet. The assessor and the remediator must be separate, a written plan comes first and a clearance check follows the work. Insurers often want to see those documents when mold is a large part of a claim. Owners may work on their own property.

We handle moisture, drying and repair. We do not perform assessments or clearance testing. See mold remediation for how we coordinate with licensed professionals.

What not to do.

  • Do not paint over mold and call it fixed.
  • Do not make permanent repairs before the insurer inspects, as the Department of Financial Services advises.
  • Do not wait for weeks to report a loss.
  • Do not assume you are covered or not covered without reading the policy.

If your claim is denied or reduced.

Ask for the reason in writing, review the policy language and provide additional documentation. The Department of Financial Services handles consumer complaints about insurers in New York. Keep your records organized.

Prevention is the best coverage.

Mold claims are hard and expensive. Fixing leaks quickly, drying wet materials in a couple of days and keeping humidity in check reduce the chance of needing one. If a loss happens, call a restoration crew early. See basement water damage for one place where drying speed matters.

A sample timeline for a mold-related claim.

A typical sequence starts with the water event and the first report to the insurer. Mitigation follows, with extraction and drying. If mold is found, a licensed assessor writes a plan, a licensed remediator performs the work and a clearance check follows. Repairs come last. Documenting each stage with dates, reports and invoices keeps the file consistent.

Delays are where mold claims go wrong. If water sits for weeks, an insurer may argue that the mold came from neglect rather than the covered event. Quick drying and prompt reporting protect both the building and the claim.

Questions to ask before you sign anything.

  • What is the policy limit for mold and does it include testing and remediation costs?
  • Does the insurer require a specific assessor or can I choose a licensed one?
  • Will the insurer pay for repairs after clearance or only for removal?
  • What happens if the area is larger than first estimated?

Mold in rentals, condos and shared buildings.

Mold claims get complicated when more than one party is involved. A tenant's renter policy generally covers belongings, while the landlord's policy covers the structure. In a condo, the association's master policy and the owner's policy each cover different parts of the unit. Who is responsible often depends on where the water started.

If you are a tenant, report moisture and mold to your landlord in writing and keep a copy. If you are an owner, tell the association and your insurer right away. Dated messages and photographs protect everyone, because they show when the problem was known and what was done about it.

  • Report in writing and keep copies of every message.
  • Photograph the area with a date visible.
  • Ask who is responsible for moisture repairs under the lease or bylaws.
  • Do not disturb suspected mold more than needed until the right party has looked.
