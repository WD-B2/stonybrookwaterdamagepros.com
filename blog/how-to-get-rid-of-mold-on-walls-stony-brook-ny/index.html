How to Get Rid of Mold on Walls After a Water Leak.

When wall mold is a do-it-yourself job, when New York requires licensed professionals and how to keep it from returning.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

A small patch of surface mold on a wall can sometimes be handled by the property owner, but only after the moisture source is fixed. In New York, mold areas over 10 square feet require licensed professionals, and the assessor and remediator must be separate. If mold keeps returning, covers a large area or sits behind the wall, stop and bring in licensed help.

This guide explains when wall mold is a do-it-yourself job, how to handle it carefully and how to prevent it from returning. It does not make health claims about mold and it is not a substitute for a licensed assessor.

Fix the water first.

Mold grows where materials stay damp. Cleaning a wall without stopping the leak or condensation is a short-term fix. Look for a plumbing leak, roof leak, window seepage, high indoor humidity or a wet floor wicking moisture upward.

If water damage caused the mold, the wall may need drying or removal before cleaning makes sense. See our page on wall and drywall water damage repair.

Is it a small job or a big one.

The New York State Department of Labor runs the mold program, and it offers a lookup tool to check that a contractor is licensed. Owners may work on their own property.

If you decide to clean a small area yourself.

Careful steps for a small surface patch.

  • Fix the leak or moisture source first..
  • Keep the area ventilated and keep children and pets away..
  • Wear gloves, eye protection and a well-fitting respirator suited to the job..
  • Wipe with detergent and water instead of spraying or scrubbing in a way that throws dust into the air..
  • Dry the area fully, and discard sponges and rags in a sealed bag..

Do not paint over mold. Paint hides the problem and traps moisture. If the wall is soft, crumbly or stained through the paint, the drywall may have to be replaced.

When to stop and call a professional.

  • The patch is larger than about 10 square feet or you are not sure.
  • The wall is wet, soft or bulging.
  • Mold returns after cleaning.
  • The musty smell is strong with no visible source.
  • Someone in the home has health concerns and wants guidance from a doctor.

What licensed remediation involves.

For larger areas, a licensed assessor inspects and writes a remediation plan. A separate licensed remediator performs the work, often using containment to keep spores from spreading. After the work, the assessor performs a clearance check. Our role is the moisture and repair side: stopping the water, drying the structure and rebuilding. See mold remediation for how we fit in.

Prevent mold coming back.

  • Repair leaks right away..
  • Dry wet materials within a couple of days..
  • Run bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans..
  • Keep indoor humidity in check..
  • Replace wet insulation and carpet pad that cannot be dried..

If wall damage came from a ceiling or pipe leak, see ceiling water damage repair and burst pipe cleanup.

Why scrubbing alone often fails.

Mold on a wall is usually the visible part of a moisture problem. Drywall paper and the insulation behind it can hold water, and the growth can extend beyond what you see on the surface. Scrubbing the surface may remove the stain without addressing the damp material behind it, so the patch returns in a few weeks.

A moisture meter is the right tool for the next step. If the reading in the wall is higher than in a dry reference spot, the problem is still active. Fix the cause and dry the wall before you decide how to treat the surface.

What to do about painted-over or recurring patches.

Walls that have been painted over several times and keep staining often have a hidden source, such as a slow plumbing leak, a window that lets in rain or a cold exterior wall where humid indoor air condenses. Look at the pattern of the stain. A ring that grows after storms points to the outside, and a stain that tracks with shower use points to plumbing.

When the cause is not obvious, ask a plumber or a building professional to help. If the wall is soft or the stain is widespread, it is time to open it, dry it and let a licensed assessor advise on what comes next.

What to expect from a repair after the mold is dealt with.

Once the moisture is fixed and any licensed work is complete, the wall still needs to be put back together. Wet or stained drywall is cut out and replaced, seams are taped and finished, and the wall is primed and painted. Use a stain-blocking primer if any staining remains on materials that were kept.

Plan on checking the area for a few weeks. If the surface stays dry and the smell is gone, the repair is holding. If you see new staining or the odor returns, there may still be a moisture source to find.

Sources and standards.

This guide draws on the references below. Check them directly for the current rules that apply to you.

  • New York State Department of Labor mold program: licensing, written remediation plans and clearance checks.
  • ANSI/IICRC S520, the published standard for professional mold remediation, cited as an industry reference.
