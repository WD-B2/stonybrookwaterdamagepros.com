Water Damage Categories: Clean, Gray and Black Water.

The three water damage categories, how to tell them apart and what each one means for cleanup.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Water damage is grouped into three categories by how contaminated the water is. Category 1 is clean water from a supply source. Category 2, often called gray water, has some contamination. Category 3, called black water, is grossly contaminated and includes sewage and floodwater from the ground. The category decides what can be dried and what must be removed.

The classification comes from the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration. This post explains the categories in plain language and what each one means for your belongings.

The three categories side by side.

Water damage categories.
Category.Common name.Typical sources.Typical handling.
Category 1.Clean water.Broken supply line, tub overflow with clean water, rain through a roof.Dry in place when caught quickly.
Category 2.Gray water.Dishwasher or washing machine discharge, toilet overflow with urine only.Clean and dry, remove porous materials as needed.
Category 3.Black water.Sewage, floodwater, rising groundwater.Remove porous materials, clean and disinfect hard surfaces.

Category 1: clean water.

Clean water comes from a sanitary source, such as a pipe or a faucet. It can still ruin a floor and a wall, but it does not carry significant contamination at the start. Carpet and drywall can often be dried if the response is fast.

Time matters. Clean water that sits for a couple of days can pick up contamination from the materials it soaks and can move to a higher category. Mold risk grows as well. See water extraction for how quickly standing water is removed.

Category 2: gray water.

Gray water has contaminants that could cause discomfort if people touch it or ingest it. Typical sources are washing machine and dishwasher overflows. Porous materials that soaked gray water are often removed, while hard surfaces are cleaned. See appliance leak damage.

Category 3: black water.

Black water carries harmful contaminants. Sewage backups and floodwater from outside the building are the usual sources. Carpet, pad, insulation and affected drywall are removed, and hard surfaces are cleaned and disinfected. Crews wear protective equipment. See sewage backup cleanup and flood damage restoration.

How a category changes over time.

A category is not fixed. Standing water can degrade, especially in warm, enclosed spaces. A clean-water loss that goes untreated for several days may be treated as a higher category when it is evaluated. That is why a quick start is the best way to keep a loss manageable.

What the category means for your belongings.

What you can do.

  • Stay out of the water if you suspect it is Category 2 or 3..
  • Photograph the source and the water before cleanup..
  • Tell the crew what the water looked and smelled like..
  • Do not use household fans or vacuums on suspected contaminated water..
  • Ask the crew which category they assign and why..

Mold assessment and clearance are separate from water categories. See mold remediation for the New York rules.

Why the standard classifies water at all.

Classification gives everyone the same language. A crew, an insurer and a property owner can talk about a Category 3 loss and know that porous materials are likely to be removed, protective equipment is needed and the cleanup will involve more than drying. Without a shared vocabulary, scopes and prices are hard to compare.

The standard also separates category from class. Category describes how contaminated the water is. Class describes how much water and how many materials are affected, from small losses to losses where deep materials hold water. A loss has both a category and a class, and each shapes the plan.

Questions to ask the crew on site.

  • What category do you assign and why?.
  • Has the category changed because of how long the water sat?.
  • Which materials are you removing because of contamination?.
  • What protective measures are you using for my family and your crew?.
  • How will you document the category in your records?.

A clear answer to these questions is a good sign. If a company cannot explain the category it assigned, ask for the reasoning in writing.

Common misunderstandings about water categories.

  • Clean water is not always safe. It can ruin materials and, if left for days, can become contaminated.
  • Gray water is not harmless. It carries some contamination and needs careful handling.
  • Black water does not always look dirty. A source such as sewage or floodwater decides the category, not the color.
  • Rain through a roof is usually clean at first, but it can pick up contaminants from roofing and attic insulation as it travels.

When in doubt, treat the water as one category higher until a crew assesses it. It is safer to be careful than to guess wrong.
