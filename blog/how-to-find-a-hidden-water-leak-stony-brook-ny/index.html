How to Find a Hidden Water Leak in Your Home.

A practical checklist for finding leaks you cannot see, from the water meter test to wall and floor clues.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Start with the water meter test. Turn off every fixture and appliance, note the meter reading and check it again after an hour or two without using water. If the reading changed, water is going somewhere. Then look for stains, soft spots, warm floor patches, sounds and musty smells. When you cannot find the source, a plumber can use listening and pressure tools to locate it.

Hidden leaks can run for weeks before they show. This guide walks through simple checks you can do yourself and the signs that mean it is time to call a plumber and a restoration crew.

Step 1: The water meter test.

  1. 1

    Stop all water use.

    Turn off faucets, appliances and irrigation. Make sure the ice maker is not running.

    .
  2. 2

    Read the meter.

    Note the reading or the position of the small leak indicator.

    .
  3. 3

    Wait.

    Leave everything off for an hour or two.

    .
  4. 4

    Read again.

    If the number changed or the indicator moved, there is a leak.

    .

If the meter does not change, the leak may be intermittent, such as a fixture that leaks only when it is used, or the issue may not be a supply leak at all.

Step 2: Check the usual suspects.

  • Toilet flapper and fill valve. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and watch whether it appears in the bowl.
  • Faucets and showerheads that drip when off.
  • Washing machine hoses and valves.
  • Water heater base and relief valve.
  • Refrigerator ice maker line.
  • Under sinks, where small drips often hide.

Step 3: Look and feel for signs.

Stains and discoloration.

Yellow or brown marks on walls and ceilings, often with a ring.

Soft or bubbling surfaces.

Drywall that gives, paint that lifts, floors that feel spongy.

Warm spots.

A patch of floor that feels warm may mean a hot water line is leaking beneath. See slab leak water damage.

Musty smell.

Damp insulation or framing creates odor with no visible source.

Step 4: Listen.

At night, with the house quiet and all fixtures off, listen for hissing, dripping or running water in walls and floors. A faint sound near a wall or under a slab is a clue. A plumber can use listening devices to pinpoint it.

Step 5: Use your bill and your eyes outside.

A water bill that rises with no change in habits can mean a leak. Walk the outside of the house too. Check hose bibs, irrigation lines and spots where the ground stays wet or the grass is greener than the rest.

When to call a restoration crew.

If you find water in a wall, ceiling or floor system, the leak needs fixing and the materials need drying. A crew uses moisture meters and thermal cameras to map the wet area, then removes or dries what is affected. The plumber fixes the source first or at the same time.

Slow leaks are a mold risk. Our mold remediation page explains what happens if growth is found. See structural drying for how drying is measured.

Keep a record.

Photograph stains, the meter readings and any plumber notes. If you file an insurance claim, a sudden event and a long-term leak are often treated differently, so dates and evidence matter. The New York State Department of Financial Services recommends documenting damage before cleanup.

Prevent future hidden leaks.

  • Replace old rubber washer hoses with braided steel hoses..
  • Know where your main shutoff is and test it yearly..
  • Check under sinks and around the water heater every few months..
  • Consider leak sensors in basements and near appliances..

Thermal cameras and moisture meters: what they show.

A moisture meter reads moisture in drywall, wood and some other materials by touching the surface or by sensing a few inches below it. It tells you whether a spot is wetter than a dry reference spot. A thermal camera shows temperature differences. A wet area often looks cooler because water evaporates and absorbs heat, so it can point toward moisture behind a finished wall.

Neither tool replaces a plumber for locating a pressurized leak, but together they map how far the damage has spread. They also help confirm when drying is done.

Leaks that only show up sometimes.

Some leaks appear only when a fixture is used, such as a shower pan that drips when someone showers or a drain line that leaks when a washer empties. Others show up only when it rains, such as a roof or window leak. Keep a simple log of when you see staining or dampness and what was happening at the time. That pattern often points to the source faster than guessing.

Common mistakes when hunting for a leak.

  • Assuming the stain is directly under the source. Water travels along pipes and framing before it shows.
  • Opening a wall without a moisture reading first, which can create a bigger repair than the leak.
  • Ignoring a small meter movement. Slow leaks matter.
  • Fixing the visible drip and not checking for older damage behind it.
