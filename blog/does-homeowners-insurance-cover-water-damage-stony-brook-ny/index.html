Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?

How sudden leaks, flooding and sewer backup are usually treated under New York policies, and what to do first.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Often yes for sudden and accidental water damage, often no for flooding and long-term seepage. Standard homeowners policies commonly cover a pipe that bursts or an appliance that fails suddenly, but they generally exclude flood damage, and many exclude damage that builds up slowly over time. Your policy wording decides, so the cause of the water matters more than the amount of it.

This guide explains how those categories usually work, what New York regulators advise and what to do before you file. It is general information and not legal or insurance advice. Your agent and your policy are the final word.

Sudden leaks versus gradual damage.

Insurers tend to separate water losses into those that happen suddenly and those that develop over time. A supply line that splits and floods a kitchen is a sudden event. A pipe that weeps inside a wall for months is usually treated as maintenance or long-term leakage, which many policies exclude.

That is why speed and evidence matter. If you can show when a problem started and what caused it, the claim has a clearer basis. Photographs, plumber notes and drying logs all help. See our guide on water extraction to understand what records a crew should keep.

Flood is different.

The New York State Department of Financial Services states that flood damage is generally covered only by flood insurance, which is usually written through the National Flood Insurance Program administered by FEMA. A standard homeowners policy does not stand in for it. If water enters from outside the building at ground level, you will likely need to look at flood coverage rather than your homeowners policy.

To understand flood exposure for a property, check the FEMA flood map. If you are unsure whether you carry flood coverage, your agent can tell you.

What to do before you file.

  • Stop the water source if it is safe to do so..
  • Photograph and video the damage before cleanup starts..
  • Make only temporary repairs until the insurer inspects, as the Department of Financial Services advises..
  • Keep damaged items when you can and keep receipts for emergency costs..
  • File the claim promptly and keep a log of who you spoke with and when..

Temporary repairs include stopping a leak, extracting standing water and protecting the property from more damage. Waiting for an adjuster before doing any of that can make the loss bigger, and most policies expect you to limit further damage.

How restoration records fit into a claim.

A restoration crew can help by recording what it finds. Useful records include photographs of the affected areas, moisture readings, a description of what was removed and the drying equipment used. Those records show the starting condition and the progress of the work. They do not decide coverage, and you decide how to work with your insurer.

Ask any company you hire what documentation it provides. Our page on structural drying explains the daily readings that typically make up a drying log.

When the insurer and the contractor disagree.

Disagreements over scope are common. An insurer may approve fewer materials than a contractor recommends, or approve a different drying period. Keep calm, ask for the reasoning in writing and provide the moisture readings and photographs that support the scope. The Department of Financial Services handles consumer complaints about insurers in New York if you feel a claim is being handled unfairly.

Questions to ask your agent today.

  • Do I have flood coverage, and what does it exclude?
  • Is sewer or drain backup covered, and up to what limit?
  • What is my deductible for water losses?
  • Does the policy expect me to keep the heat on or turn off the water when I travel?
  • What documentation do you require to open a claim?

Deductibles, limits and what is actually paid.

Even when a loss is covered, the payout is shaped by your deductible, your coverage limits and how the insurer values damaged materials. Some policies pay the cost to repair or replace, and others pay depreciated value first. Ask your agent which method your policy uses, because it changes how much you receive for carpet, flooring and cabinets.

Emergency steps such as extraction and drying are usually treated as part of preventing further loss. Keep invoices and the equipment log, and ask whether the insurer wants to approve a scope before larger repairs begin. Clear communication early avoids disputes later.

Renters, condo owners and landlords.

If you rent, your landlord's policy covers the building and your renter's policy covers your belongings, so a leak can involve two insurers. Condo owners often have a master policy for the building and an individual policy for interior finishes and contents. Landlords need to know who is responsible for what under the lease. In each case, report the loss in writing, photograph everything and keep copies of every message.

For multi-unit buildings, a leak that started in one unit can reach several others. Recording where the water began and where it traveled helps each party and each insurer see the same facts.
