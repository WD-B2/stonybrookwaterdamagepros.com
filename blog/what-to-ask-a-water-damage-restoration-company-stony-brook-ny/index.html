What to Ask a Water Damage Restoration Company.

Questions that reveal how a restoration company works, from scope and pricing to equipment and records.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Ask how the company will scope the job, what it measures, what it records and how it explains price. A good restoration company answers in plain language, explains the steps before it starts and puts the scope in writing. Be cautious of anyone who quotes a firm total over the phone, pushes you to sign immediately or cannot explain how they know when a building is dry.

Use these questions when you are choosing help after a leak, flood or storm. They apply to any company, including ours.

Questions about scope and price.

  • Will you explain the scope and the cost factors before work starts?.
  • What is included, such as extraction, drying and monitoring, and what is separate?.
  • How do you handle surprises, like hidden moisture or contaminated water?.
  • Do you quote a number over the phone? A responsible company usually will not..
  • How do you charge for equipment and monitoring days?.

Water damage jobs vary, so price follows scope. We describe the cost factors on every service page, such as the one for water extraction, because a similar leak can be a very different job once it is opened up.

Questions about how they measure.

  • Do you use moisture meters and thermal imaging?
  • How will you know the building is dry?
  • Do you provide daily readings?
  • What equipment will you use and where will it go?

A company that cannot explain how it measures is guessing. Drying should end on readings that match a dry reference. See structural drying for the method.

Questions about standards and licensing.

What to ask and what a good answer sounds like.
Question.A good answer.A warning sign.
Which standards do you follow?Explains ANSI/IICRC S500 as a reference.Claims certification with no detail.
Are you licensed and insured?Gives clear answers and documents.Vague or defensive.
Who handles mold?Explains New York requires licensed, separate assessors and remediators for larger areas.Offers to assess and remediate large areas themselves.
Do you repair plumbing?Says a plumber handles pipes.Promises everything in one visit.

Questions about records and insurance.

  • What photos and logs will you give me?.
  • Can you share them with my insurer?.
  • Will you separate mitigation from repair in the scope?.
  • Do you tell me what you recommend and why?.

The New York State Department of Financial Services advises policyholders to file claims promptly, photograph damage before cleanup and make only temporary repairs until the insurer inspects. A company that understands that will keep its work aligned with it.

Questions about timing.

  • How is dispatch handled at night and on weekends?
  • When can a crew arrive? Be wary of absolute promises. Same-day response is often offered when available.
  • How long will drying take and what affects that?

Red flags.

  • Pressure to sign a contract before the scope is explained.
  • A demand to remove everything with no moisture readings.
  • No explanation of water type.
  • No written scope.
  • A claim that can handle plumbing, roofing, mold assessment and rebuilding without any licensed trades.

What a good first call sounds like.

A good dispatcher asks where the water is, whether it is still running, what it touched and whether it looks or smells contaminated. They help with safe first steps and explain what happens next. They do not make promises they cannot keep.

See our about page for how we work.

How to compare two scopes side by side.

Line up the scopes and compare the same items. Look at whether extraction, drying equipment, monitoring visits and removal of materials are included. Check how each scope treats contents, cleaning and repair. A lower total can mean fewer monitoring days or a smaller removal list, so ask what is missing rather than assuming the lower number is the better deal.

Ask each company to explain the largest line item in plain language. If the explanation is clear and tied to what they found in the building, that is a good sign.

Questions for commercial and multi-unit properties.

  • Can you work in zones so unaffected areas stay open?
  • How will you share records with managers, tenants and insurers?
  • How do you handle after-hours work and building access?
  • Who coordinates with other trades such as electricians and plumbers?

What to have ready before you call.

  • Photos of the damage and the source..
  • Your insurer's claim number if you have one..
  • A rough idea of how long the water sat..
  • The names of other contractors, such as a plumber, who are already involved..

Having these details ready lets the company give you a useful first answer, and it signals that you are paying attention to the process.

Follow-up questions after the job.

  • Can I have a copy of the readings and photos?
  • What should I watch for in the next few weeks?
  • Who do I call if odor, staining or dampness returns?
  • What repairs remain and who handles them?

Trust your own read of the conversation.

Pay attention to how the company talks to you. Do they answer your questions directly, explain terms and admit what they do not know? Do they respect your decision to take time with a larger choice? A company that treats you as a partner in the process is usually a better fit than one that treats the call as a sales opportunity.

Write down what you are told and by whom. If a later bill or scope does not match, you will have a record to compare.

Sources and standards.

This guide draws on the references below. Check them directly for the current rules that apply to you.

  • ANSI/IICRC S500, the published standard for professional water damage restoration, cited as an industry reference and not as a claim of certification.
  • New York State Department of Financial Services consumer guidance on filing and documenting water damage claims.
