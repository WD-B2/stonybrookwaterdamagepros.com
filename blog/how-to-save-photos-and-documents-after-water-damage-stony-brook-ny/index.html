How to Save Photos and Documents After Water Damage.

First-hours steps for wet photographs, paper records and books, and when to get professional help.

24/7 dispatch line.

(934) 223-1875.

Call now.

By the Stony Brook Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026-11-12.

The short answer.

Act within the first day or two. Do not separate stuck photos by force. Rinse off dirt gently with clean water if needed, then air-dry items flat on clean paper or cloth, or freeze them to stop damage until you can get help. Wet paper and photographs grow mold quickly, so the faster you stabilize them the better the odds.

This guide gives step-by-step handling for photographs, books and papers and explains when to ask a conservator or a specialized drying service.

First hour and first day.

Priority steps.

  • Make sure the area is safe and the source of water is stopped..
  • Wear gloves if the water may be contaminated..
  • Do not try to peel apart stuck photos or pages..
  • Move items to a cool, dry spot with good airflow..
  • If you cannot dry them within a day or two, freeze them in sealed bags..

Photographs.

  1. 1

    Rinse gently.

    If dirty, hold under clean running water and do not touch the image surface.

    .
  2. 2

    Separate carefully.

    Slide apart gently in water if they are stuck. If they resist, freeze them and get help.

    .
  3. 3

    Dry flat.

    Place face up on clean paper towels or cloth, away from sun.

    .
  4. 4

    Replace towels.

    Change absorbent paper as it gets wet.

    .

Documents and books.

Handling by item.
Item.Best first step.Avoid.
Loose papers.Air-dry flat or interleave with blotting paper.Pulling apart stuck sheets.
Books.Stand upright, fan pages gently, then dry.Closing wet books.
Photographs.Air-dry flat or freeze.Direct sunlight or heat.
Important records.Make copies, then dry or freeze originals.Throwing away without copies.
Ink-jet prints and newer papers.Handle carefully, ink may run.Rubbing wet surfaces.

What to do with originals you need.

Birth certificates, deeds, wills and tax records are irreplaceable or time-consuming to replace. Photocopy or scan them as soon as they are dry enough, freeze the originals if you need time and contact the issuing office about replacements if needed.

Digital copies and backups.

Scan or photograph precious documents as part of your recovery. Store copies in the cloud and on a separate drive. For next time, keep a copy of critical papers outside the house.

Mold risk.

Paper and photographs are food for mold. If you see growth, do not clean it on your own and consider freezing the items and seeking professional guidance. See mold remediation for how New York rules apply to buildings, and keep items away from the damp area.

What a restoration company can do.

Some restoration companies offer contents pack-out, document drying and cleaning. Others work with specialists. Ask what is available and what it costs. For the building, see water extraction and structural drying.

Insurance notes.

Document what was damaged, what it was worth and what you tried to save. The New York State Department of Financial Services recommends keeping an inventory and a log. Photograph items before you dry or discard them.

Prevent the loss next time.

  • Store papers in waterproof containers and off the floor.
  • Keep copies of key documents outside the home.
  • Avoid storing photos in basements and garages.
  • Use plastic bins with lids for sentimental items.

Freezing as a way to buy time.

Freezing stops mold growth and slows further damage, which buys time while you arrange professional drying. Place items in sealed bags, ideally with sheets of freezer paper between photographs or pages. Label the bags and keep them upright if possible. A home freezer works for small collections.

Freezing is not a cure, but it lets you handle the rest of the cleanup without rushing. When you are ready, thaw items slowly and dry them as described above or hand them to a specialist.

Albums, scrapbooks and framed pieces.

Albums and scrapbooks are hard to dry because pages stick together. Take photos out of albums only if they are not stuck. Framed pieces should be opened and the glass removed so the print can dry. If glass is stuck to the surface, freeze it and consult a conservator.

Handling specific materials.

Photo and paper types and what they need.
Type.What to know.Handling tip.
Color prints.Emulsion can stick to other surfaces when wet.Dry face up and never face down on a flat surface.
Black and white prints.Generally hold up better but still stick.Separate in clean water while wet.
Negatives and slides.Gelatin can stick and scratch.Rinse gently and hang to dry.
Coated or glossy paper.Pages fuse together as they dry.Freeze and seek professional help.
Newspaper and clippings.Fragile and acidic.Photocopy, then dry carefully.

If an item matters a great deal to you, do not experiment on the only copy. A conservator or a document recovery service can often handle pieces you are not comfortable touching.

Decide what is worth saving.

Saving everything can be a lot of work in a stressful week. Start with irreplaceable items such as family photographs and legal papers, then move to items that are costly to replace. Common printed books and ordinary mail are often easier to replace than to dry.

A short list helps. Write down the items you most want to save and the order you will handle them. That makes it easier to decide quickly when you have limited freezer space or time.

Sources and standards.

This guide draws on the references below. Check them directly for the current rules that apply to you.

  • ANSI/IICRC S500, the published standard for professional water damage restoration, cited as an industry reference and not as a claim of certification.
  • New York State Department of Financial Services consumer guidance on filing and documenting water damage claims.
