Removing visible water is only half the job. Structural drying pulls moisture out of framing, subfloors and walls with measured equipment until readings are back to normal.

Signs your building is still wet after the water is gone.

A musty smell that lingers.

Dampness trapped in insulation or framing keeps producing odor after surfaces look dry.

Floors that cup or feel soft.

Wood and subfloor absorb water and release it slowly.

Paint that bubbles or stains return.

Moisture moving through drywall pushes through the finish.

Window condensation indoors.

High indoor humidity after a water loss is a sign the structure is still releasing moisture.

If you notice any of these after a leak or flood, the structure may still hold water. A meter reading tells you more than the surface does.

What structural drying actually does.

Wet materials release moisture into the air as vapor. If that vapor stays in the room, the air becomes saturated and the materials stop drying. Drying equipment breaks that cycle. Air movers push air across wet surfaces to lift moisture. Dehumidifiers pull that moisture out of the air and out of the building.

We aim for a controlled drying environment, then measure. Moisture meters and thermal cameras show which materials are still wet. Readings are compared with a dry reference so the job ends on data, not a guess.

How a drying job runs day by day.

  1. 1

    Set up.

    Equipment is placed based on moisture mapping and the volume of the affected space.

    .
  2. 2

    Monitor daily.

    We read moisture levels and adjust air movers and dehumidifiers as materials dry.

    .
  3. 3

    Move or remove.

    Items that block airflow, such as baseboards or a section of drywall, may be removed.

    .
  4. 4

    Confirm dry.

    When readings reach the dry standard, we remove equipment and record final values.

    .

Many jobs take several days. Large or deeply saturated structures take longer. We explain the plan and the expected equipment time before we start.

What you can expect during drying.

  • Equipment makes noise and uses electricity. Fans run around the clock for the first days.
  • The affected area can feel warm and dry. That is part of the process.
  • Do not move or turn off equipment. If you need a circuit for something else, call us.
  • Plan for crew visits to read meters each day.

Documentation that supports a claim.

Drying logs record equipment placement, daily readings and the final results. The New York Department of Financial Services recommends keeping thorough records and photographs when you file an insurance claim. We keep our logs clear so you can share them with your insurer.
